# INTEVARIS — Stage 6: Healthcare and Legal Assistance Market Discovery

**Research cut-off / source access date:** 11 November 2026

**Scope:** United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Australia; healthcare/community organizations and legal assistance organizations considered separately.

**Purpose:** Public-source discovery of current interpretation-service workflows, operational frictions, buyers, procurement and alternatives. This is not product validation, legal advice, a market-size estimate, a sales forecast, or a customer list.

## 1. Executive findings

**Bottom line:** Both segments have documented, consequential interpretation workflows, but the evidence does not support selecting one as the winner. Healthcare has more routinely urgent, multi-modality coordination across clinical settings and clear safety/governance responsibilities. Legal assistance has highly sensitive, case-linked communication and more visibly fragmented funding and expense-recovery rules. In both segments, mature agencies, government programs and manual workarounds already cover much of the basic booking function.

- **VERIFIED FACT —** Public healthcare workflows range from a single staff member calling a vendor to centralized hospital interpreter departments, regional health-authority dispatch, and a hospital electronic-record-driven scheduling process. They are not one standardized workflow. NHS England’s 2025 framework assigns commissioning responsibility to local NHS trusts and integrated care boards; Manitoba’s Shared Health runs centralized 24/7 intake; the Royal Children’s Hospital Melbourne describes EMR-driven scheduling and exception handling. [S6, S13, S20]
- **VERIFIED FACT —** Legal assistance models also differ materially. Ayuda’s Washington, DC Community Legal Interpreter Bank schedules and bills assignments for a network of public-interest legal providers; Legal Aid NSW’s published CLCP policy describes a centralized TIS National billing account and reimbursement procedures, but its scheduled review date has passed and completion is not confirmed; Legal Aid Ontario’s April 2026 handbook sets reimbursement rules for eligible interpreter work. [S26, S30, S33, S34, S44]
- **VERIFIED FACT —** There is evidence of real spending and procurement, but it is uneven. Legal Aid NSW reported **A$2,181,108.18, excluding GST, spent on interpreting and translation in FY2024–25**. UCLH’s published 2024 award notice reports a **£4 million** interpretation and translation contract for 2024–28. A 2025 NHS SBS framework award is published at **£73 million**, but a framework ceiling/value is not equivalent to expenditure by users. These are not evidence of willingness to buy INTEVARIS. [S9, S10, S27]
- **REPORTED —** Public documents describe failures and burdens, but do not quantify a cross-country software-addressable problem. NHS England’s 2025 framework cites maternity investigations where professional interpretation was not documented in 73% of reviewed cases and no interpretation was used in 50%; this is a specific reviewed-case finding, not a universal NHS rate. The same framework reports two local operational case studies—Nottingham University Hospitals (NUH) and Hampshire and Isle of Wight ICB—summarized below; those figures are source-reported and not national benchmarks. [S6] Legal aid providers responding to a UK government consultation reported difficulty finding suitably qualified interpreters at short notice and that arranging them consumed caseworker time. [S6, S35]
- **INFERENCE —** A coordination layer could be relevant where service demand crosses departments, funding rules, suppliers and systems, or where staff cannot see the request-to-completion trail. The evidence does not establish that any named organization lacks adequate tools, that existing vendor portals fail its requirements, or that a new system would be adopted.
- **UNKNOWN —** Actual cancellation/no-show rates, late-change burden, unfilled requests, elapsed time spent by coordinators, completeness of session records, current software/system integrations, and software budget ownership are generally not public. Interviews and workflow observation are necessary.

**No market or segment is selected as a winner.** The strongest next step is a small, consent-based discovery program with operational, budget and privacy stakeholders in both segments—starting with organizations whose public material exposes a concrete workflow, but not presuming that they are customers or interview participants.

## 2. Research method, evidence labels and limitations

### Method

I reviewed official organization service pages and policies, public procurement notices and contracts, annual reports, regulator/government material and public vendor documentation. Evidence was sought for each country and each segment. Ten organizations below were chosen because official sources independently establish a relevant service and an interpretation/language-access connection. Links, titles and access dates appear in the source register.

### Evidence labels

- **VERIFIED FACT:** directly established by the cited official or otherwise reliable source.
- **REPORTED:** a source or organization states something that has not been independently confirmed here (including self-reported operations or consultation responses).
- **INFERENCE:** a reasoned interpretation of cited evidence, not a directly measured fact.
- **ESTIMATE:** an approximation with an explicit method. No market-size or price estimates are made in this report.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** an untested proposition requiring customer validation.
- **UNKNOWN:** evidence is insufficient or unavailable publicly.

### Limitations

This is desk research, not customer research. There were no interviews, site visits, vendor demonstrations, procurement-team conversations, or validation of internal procedures. Public policy documents can be outdated, aspirational, or not followed in every encounter. The Legal Aid NSW CLCP Interpreter Policy says it was scheduled for review by 30 June 2025; the public CLC page still lists the August 2024 PDF, but reviewed sources do not confirm the review was completed. Its provisions are described as policy text, not independently verified November 2026 operating practice. A procurement notice may be a notice of intent, tender, framework, or award; these states are kept distinct. Contract values may be maximum/framework amounts, not drawdown or realized spend. Annual-report spend is reported expenditure for the stated category and period, not a product budget. Vendor claims are identified as vendor claims. Legal/privacy material is summarized for discovery only and is not legal advice.

Country comparisons are not national market-size estimates. Within-country variation is material, particularly among US states and providers, Canadian provinces and health authorities, UK nations and NHS purchasers, and Australian states/territories and federally funded programs.

### Country-level implications

- **INFERENCE —** The UK and Australia show clearer public frameworks or funding schemes, but they also provide established platforms, suppliers, public programs or pooled accounts that may reduce demand for a separate coordinator tool. [S10, S14, S22–S26]
- **INFERENCE —** The US may offer more institution-specific buying points, yet discovery must distinguish system procurement from provider-by-provider procurement and avoid treating an RFP as an award. [S4]
- **INFERENCE —** Canada presents a particularly important province/region segmentation question: a regional centralized program can look very different from a community clinic that buys or accesses a third-party network. [S13–S19]
- **HYPOTHESIS —** Cross-organization interoperability and allocation visibility may be more valuable than a new standalone interpreter directory where a government-funded service or incumbent agency already handles dispatch. This requires testing with coordinators and procurement stakeholders.

## 4. Healthcare and community segment analysis

### Segment boundary

This segment includes hospitals/networks, community health centres, primary care, and community health/social-care providers. The examples include a hospital network, NHS acute trust, two Canadian community/regional health settings, and a children’s hospital. Do not assume their budgets, patient flows or statutory duties are interchangeable.

### Observed operating patterns

1. **Request identification and intake:** A patient may disclose a preferred language to a care team; NHS primary-care guidance recommends recording language/communication needs and flagging/sharing them. The Royal Children’s Hospital (RCH) describes an EMR registration flag as the trigger for interpreter scheduling reports. [S7, S20]

2. **Triage and review:** At UCLH, the patient asks the attending department and hospital staff arrange access; the patient cannot contact the vendor directly. At Shared Health Manitoba, health providers request the interpreter on behalf of the patient through 24/7 central dispatch or form-based channels. [S8, S13]
3. **Sourcing and assignment:** Models documented include contracted language-service agencies (UCLH), a mixed staff-interpreter plus contracted telephone pool (Shared Health), and hospital interpreter departments/agency fallback (RCH). Qualifications range from NHS-commissioner recommendations and trust criteria to RCH’s stated NAATI context. [S8, S11, S13, S20, S21]
4. **Urgency:** Immediate telephone interpretation is available through UCLH’s stated provider and Shared Health’s 24/7 channels. RCH says a phone interpreter may be requested at any time; under-48-hour needs require contacting its office, and out-of-hours face-to-face in extreme circumstances requires nursing-hospital-manager approval. [S8, S13, S20]
5. **Changes, cancellations and missed appointments:** RCH’s workflow indicates a distinction between EMR and non-EMR appointments; Shared Health’s interpreters can make reminder calls two business days before an appointment. Public material reviewed did not establish a standard cancellation, reassignment, late-change, or no-show process across these organizations. [S13, S20]
6. **Qualification and language checks:** NHS guidance calls for registered/experienced interpreters and advises against reliance on family or unqualified interpreters. RCH references NAATI accreditation and the need for an appropriate language/dialect/gender match. NYC Health + Hospitals’ RFP requires vendor evidence of qualifications and asks for responsiveness and staffing information; this is a requirement sought in a solicitation, not proof of vendor delivery. [S7, S21, S4]
7. **Completion and outcome records:** NHS trust policy places invoice verification with divisional management; RCH uses an EMR order and its scheduling-report flow; HHS’s sample procedures recommend documenting interpreter use and preferred language in the patient’s record or a log. These sources do not establish a universal session-completion standard or outcome taxonomy. [S11, S20, S3]
8. **Communication:** Public examples include phone, in-person and video connection, central dispatch, booking forms, vendor portals and reminder calls. Shared Health accepts provider requests by phone, fax and email; the exact channel varies by language/service. [S8, S13]
9. **Tools and systems:** Evidence ranges from EHR/EMR-linked flags and orders (RCH; NYC RFP’s stated aspiration for Epic integration), to agency portals and telephone access, to central internal dispatch. The NYC RFP’s Epic-integration statement is an organization goal in RFP Q&A, not a delivered integration. [S20, S4, S24]
10. **Governance:** Healthcare interpretation may involve patient records, health details and vendor access. US HHS says a contracted interpreter service acting for a covered provider may be a business associate and require written assurances; Manitoba requires written agreements with information managers and restricts access by need; Australian APP 11 requires reasonable security and destruction/de-identification when no longer needed, subject to exceptions. [S2, S18, S25]

### NHS England operational case studies and alternatives

- **REPORTED — Nottingham University Hospitals (NUH):** NHS England describes a hybrid model combining in-house professional interpreters, trained volunteers, bank interpreters and external-provider backup, coordinated through an internal booking system for urgent requests and communication among interpreters, clinicians and patients. As of March 2024, the framework reports 87 interpreters, 3 administrators, 59 languages and about 25,000 bookings per year. It reports nearly £1m savings in 2015–21 compared with 2014/15 costs. These are case-study claims reported by NHS England, not independently audited here; they do not establish current 2026 staffing, scale or savings. [S6]
- **REPORTED — Hampshire and Isle of Wight ICB:** NHS England says only 101 of 1,082 eligible primary-care sites had language support in the cited review. The ICB moved to one spoken-language provider with utilization reporting and unique site IDs; the framework reports use rising from 67,375 minutes in 2022 to 265,668 in 2024. These are source-reported local measures, not independently validated or representative of all NHS providers. [S6]
- **INFERENCE —** These examples show alternatives to a standalone coordination product: internal booking systems and in-house interpreter pools, incumbent-provider utilization reports, and site-level identifiers. Existing arrangements may cover some coordination needs or make integration more appropriate. Neither case establishes universal NHS adoption or direct international transferability. [S6]

### Documented operational friction (not generalized into prevalence)

- **VERIFIED FACT —** The RCH notes that first-time patients may not have an interpreter booked if the referral failed to flag the need or the patient is not fully registered in its system. It gives staff an exception path: call the office for phone interpreting, or contact the office promptly for a short-notice assignment. [S20]
- **REPORTED —** Access Alliance’s 2025–26 client experience report recommends stronger follow-up on appointment changes and test results and more consistent interpretation at intake, booking and clinical encounters. This is a service-level improvement recommendation, not proof that every appointment fails or that a particular software would solve it. [S17]
- **REPORTED —** NHS England’s national framework cites reviewed maternity cases in which interpretation use was not documented or absent, and identifies safety and confidentiality as improvement domains. The framework’s evidence is important but should not be generalized to every patient, NHS provider or encounter. [S6]
- **HYPOTHESIS —** Coordinators may lose time reconstructing whether an interpreter was requested, confirmed, joined, completed or billed when handoffs cross vendor portals and clinical records. Public policies show multiple handoffs and logging approaches, but do not measure this time cost. [S4, S11, S13, S20]

## 5. Legal assistance segment analysis

### Segment boundary

This segment includes legal aid commissions, nonprofit legal service providers, immigration legal assistance organizations and community legal clinics. Court interpreter provision is a related alternative but is not automatically a legal-aid organization’s workflow or budget.

### Observed operating patterns

1. **Request intake:** Legal need may surface in a telephone intake, clinic appointment, advice session, immigration matter, court appearance or client-document review. Praxis advertises phone advice and limited-capacity in-person drop-ins; Ayuda’s bank serves lawyer-client meetings outside court. [S28, S30]
2. **Review and authorization:** The Legal Aid NSW CLCP policy document says eligible program-funded matters should use the specified TIS National arrangement first, with approval/reimbursement paths for some other services; this is dated policy text, and completion of the scheduled review is unconfirmed. Legal Aid Ontario’s April 2026 handbook sets certificate/disbursement limits and prior-authorization rules. [S26, S34, S44]
3. **Sourcing and scheduling:** Ayuda states that its bank handles scheduling and billing for assignments. The NSW CLCP policy describes an account/client-code structure, immediate ATIS/TIS fallback and pre-booked sessions for complex, specialist or limited-language situations; current operation is not independently confirmed. Praxis describes online and telephone booking systems for its interpreting service. [S26, S29–S31, S44]
4. **Urgency:** Ayuda describes an emergency basis for some bank assignments and contracted on-demand telephonic backup; the NSW CLCP policy document describes immediate telephone options before prebooked sessions. The reviewed sources do not establish a common urgent-response target for legal clinics or confirm the NSW policy review outcome. [S26, S30, S44]
5. **Changes and missed appointments:** The dated NSW CLCP policy recommends confirming appointments and giving providers sufficient cancellation notice to avoid charges; current implementation after its scheduled review date is unconfirmed. LAO’s April 2026 handbook has two separate provisions: Fees—Cancellation (p. 23) says cancellation/missed-appointment fees are not paid by LAO and are the legally-aided client’s personal responsibility; Interpreters—Generally (p. 26) says interpreter cancellation/missed-appointment costs are overhead and not paid by LAO if billed by an interpreter. The handbook does not expressly state whether the general personal-responsibility clause applies to interpreter-specific invoices. Clarification from LAO or the relevant contract is needed for an actual charge. [S26, S34, S44]
6. **Qualification and confidentiality:** Ayuda requires foundation interpreter training and legal-context training and describes confidentiality and attorney-client privilege training and signed agreements. The NSW CLCP policy document requires NAATI-certified interpreters for separately procured/reimbursed services; current practice after the scheduled review date is not independently confirmed. [S26, S30, S31, S44]
7. **Completion and billing:** The NSW CLCP policy document describes account-level controls for eligible CLCP services, central handling of monthly account invoices and quarterly reimbursement invoices; review completion is unknown. LAO’s April 2026 handbook says interpreter accounts must be itemized with interpreter identity, dates/times and a brief service description. [S26, S34, S44]
8. **Communication:** Attorney/client meetings, telephone/video and in-person settings are documented, along with multilingual intake phone lines at LAFLA. The sources do not specify a single shared case-management or interpreter communication system across organizations. [S30–S32]
9. **Tools and alternatives:** Publicly documented alternatives include shared interpreter banks, government TIS accounts, local agency contracts, on-demand phone interpretation, bilingual staff or community-language lines, court-provided interpreters for proceedings, legal aid disbursement processes and manual approval/invoice records. [S26, S30, S32–S34]
10. **Governance:** Legal interpretation may disclose confidential client narratives, immigration status, trauma, criminal/civil case information or privileged communications. Ayuda explicitly trains interpreters on privilege/confidentiality; procurement and access rights must be assessed with counsel and privacy staff. [S30, S31]

### Documented operational friction (not generalized into prevalence)

- **PUBLISHED POLICY —** The CLCP Interpreter Policy (effective 1 August 2023; eligible-organization list updated from 1 July 2024) documents a multi-step arrangement around service eligibility, account/client code, alternate providers and reimbursement, with invoice/funding controls assigned to the CLCP Unit and participating organizations. It was scheduled for review by 30 June 2025; the public page still lists the August 2024 PDF, but no reviewed source confirms completion. Treat as policy text, not independently confirmed November 2026 practice. [S26, S44]
- **VERIFIED RULES; APPLICATION UNCERTAIN —** The April 2026 LAO handbook allows up to 10 hours for solicitor-client interpreter meetings without prior authorization and requires itemized interpreter accounts (p. 26). It also has two distinct cancellation clauses: the general Fees—Cancellation clause says cancellation/missed-appointment fees are the legally-aided client’s personal responsibility (p. 23), while the interpreter-specific clause says interpreter cancellation/missed-appointment costs are overhead and are not paid by LAO if billed by an interpreter (p. 26). The handbook does not expressly reconcile the clauses. Do not assume the general rule automatically governs interpreter charges or that the interpreter clause alone identifies the ultimate payer. Responsibility for an actual interpreter cancellation invoice requires clarification from LAO or the relevant contractual arrangement. [S34]
- **REPORTED —** UK immigration legal aid consultation respondents described short-notice interpreter availability as difficult and arranging interpreters as time-consuming; those were stakeholder reports in a consultation, not independently measured rates. [S35]
- **HYPOTHESIS —** A shared request record could reduce duplicated fact-finding and reduce billing leakage where an organization serves multiple funding agreements. This is plausible from the NSW policy’s eligibility/account controls, but no measured leakage or processing time is established. [S26]

## 11. Confidentiality, privacy and governance considerations

This section is an issue map for discovery, not legal advice or a determination of any organization’s obligations.

- **United States health:** HHS says a provider may disclose PHI to an interpreter service acting on its behalf under a business-associate arrangement, with required written assurances; HHS’s Section 1557 summary states language assistance must protect privacy and be free/accurate/timely, while also noting court stays/enjoinments affecting some provisions of the 2024 rule. Applicability and current litigation must be checked with counsel. [S1–S3]
- **United Kingdom health:** ICO guidance treats health information as special-category data and describes additional conditions, minimization, documentation and security requirements; the ICO’s general storage-limitation guidance states personal data should not be kept longer than needed except under applicable exceptions. [S38, S39]
- **Canada health:** Manitoba PHIA requires need-to-know access and written safeguards in agreements with information managers; the federal privacy commissioner describes medical information as highly sensitive and third-party safeguards as the organization’s responsibility under PIPEDA guidance. Provincial laws and institutional status vary. [S18, S40]
- **Australia health:** OAIC guidance and APP 11 call for reasonable steps to protect personal information and to destroy/de-identify information no longer needed, with statutory exceptions. Government health providers may also have additional state/territory, records and My Health Record obligations. [S25, S41]
- **Legal privilege and confidentiality:** Ayuda’s legal-interpreter training includes attorney-client privilege, confidentiality and avoiding unauthorized practice of law. This is concrete evidence that interpreter handling differs from generic customer-service interpreting. [S30, S31]
- **Access controls and data minimization (INFERENCE):** A coordination record could expose language, appointment/case metadata and client identity even if it does not store interpreted content. Discovery should determine minimum identifiers and whether booking metadata itself is sensitive. Do not store recordings/transcripts by default; validate necessity, retention and access with each organization’s governance staff.
- **Accountability questions:** Who is controller/custodian, who is a service provider/business associate/processor, what is the permitted purpose, where data resides, who can see case and health identifiers, how is interpreter access logged, how are incidents reported, what retention schedule applies, how are records exported/deleted, and how are privilege and confidentiality handled contractually? These must be answered per jurisdiction and organization before any product assumptions.

## 13. Interview questions for each segment

No interviews have been conducted. Ask about recent real examples; request permission before reviewing any workflow artifacts. Avoid client/patient identifiers and protected records.

### Healthcare/community interview guide

**For operational users and coordinators**
1. Walk me through the most recent interpreter request from first recognition of need to session closure. Which system/form/channel was used at each step?
2. How do you capture preferred language, dialect, interpreter gender, modality, accessibility needs and urgency? When is that data available to the next team?
3. Who reviews and authorizes the request? Which requests go through a central team, and which are handled by the department or individual clinician?
4. For a scheduled encounter, how do you check availability, confirm assignment and inform the patient and clinician? What changes after a cancellation or reschedule?
5. What happens for an emergency, an under-48-hour request, an uncommon language or no interpreter available? What is the escalation and fallback path?
6. How do you verify interpreter credentials, specialism, language/dialect and prior assignment suitability? Who can see that evidence?
7. How do staff record that an interpreter joined and the encounter completed? Which system is authoritative? What evidence is needed for quality review and invoice approval?
8. What are the common reasons a request is late, unfilled, duplicated or billed incorrectly? Can you describe the most recent instance without identifying a patient?
9. Which vendor portals, phone services, EMR/EHR modules, spreadsheets, request forms and internal directories do you actually use? Which are mandatory?
10. Which information must not be put into a booking system? What access, audit, retention, deletion, data-location and incident requirements apply?

**For managers, procurement, finance, privacy and IT**
11. Who controls each budget and who pays for on-demand, pre-booked, on-site, sign-language and translation work? What is reimbursed or free at point of use?
12. What current contract/framework/procurement route applies, when does it renew, and who approves a new supplier or software product?
13. Which performance measures are reviewed (fill rate, time-to-connect, cancellation, complaints, quality, cost, documentation)? Can those data be shared in aggregate?
14. What integrations, security reviews, clinical-safety assessments, accessibility tests and implementation approvals are mandatory? Who can veto a change?
15. What operational change would be valuable enough to justify switching or adding a system, and what evidence would you require before purchase? What would make you keep the current approach?

### Legal assistance interview guide

**For lawyers/caseworkers, intake staff and interpreter-bank coordinators**
1. Walk me through a recent case where language support was needed, from intake to completed lawyer-client communication. What request record or case ID was used?
2. How do you decide between staff language capability, a shared interpreter bank, TIS/phone service, a contracted interpreter, a court interpreter or another alternative?
3. How do funding source, legal matter, client eligibility, legal-aid certificate or grant restrictions affect which interpreter can be booked and paid?
4. Who can authorize a booking or a longer assignment? What approvals are required for a rare language, specialist legal terminology, urgent request or service outside the program rules?
5. What happens when an interpreter is unavailable, a client is in crisis, the meeting changes, or the client/attorney does not attend? Does the provider charge a cancellation fee, does LAO reimburse it, and—if unpaid—who ultimately bears it?
6. How do you check credentials, confidentiality commitments, legal-context training, conflicts and interpreter independence? How are concerns raised and documented?
7. How do lawyers share case context and terminology with an interpreter without oversharing? How is privilege maintained in phone, video, group or remote sessions?
8. What evidence is needed to record assignment completion, bill the legal aid authority/funder, claim reimbursement, allocate the expense to the right matter and defend an audit?
9. Which systems are used for case management, calendars, TIS/client codes, shared-bank requests, time recording, approvals and invoices? Where is data re-entered?
10. What client information is collected in an interpreter request, who may see it, and how long is it retained? How are interpreter notes or documents handled after a session?

**For program directors, finance/procurement, funders and privacy counsel**
11. Who has budget authority for interpreting, who pays the bill, and which funding pots exclude particular services or clients?
12. Is interpreter access purchased centrally, allocated through a shared bank, procured by each center/law firm, or claimed as case disbursement? Which route is most common and why?
13. What is the procurement/approval cycle and what conditions attach to grants, public funding or legal aid contracts?
14. What are the measurable pain points and how are they measured? Which are genuine coordination problems versus interpreter supply, funding or casework-capacity problems?
15. Which governance requirements are non-negotiable for confidentiality, privilege, access control, audit trail, hosting, deletion, breach response and vendor contracts?
16. What would be the smallest change that improved service without adding administrative burden? What evidence would disprove the need for a new coordination system?

## 15. Unknowns, risks and recommended next research action

### Highest-priority unknowns

1. For each prospect, what actually happens on a recent request from intake through booking, interpreter confirmation, completion, billing and record closure?
2. Which requests are unfilled, late, duplicated, changed or cancelled; how often, why, and what does each event cost in staff time, service delay or client/patient impact?
3. Which tools are actually used—including EHR/EMR, case-management, calendars, phone systems, vendor portal, TIS portal, forms, spreadsheets and finance systems—and what is integrated versus copied manually?
4. Who owns the budget and approves a new workflow or software purchase? Are language-service contracts and software contracts purchased together or separately? Which funding rules prevent pooled or shared purchase?
5. What service-level measures exist, how are they audited, and can staff retrieve request-to-session evidence without using patient/client records?
6. How do organizations verify interpreters’ qualifications, language/dialect, specialty, confidentiality agreement, screening status and conflicts? Who is accountable when a supplier changes an assignment?
7. What are the exact governance requirements for metadata, retention, access, audit, data residency, security review, privilege, cross-border processing and integrations?
8. What will happen to PHN mental-health interpretation funding after June 2028, and what FIS/health eligibility changes may be made before then?
9. In Canada, which provincial privacy, health-service and funding regimes apply to each target workflow; what service is centrally funded, and what is organization-paid?
10. For the UK, what are the actual current drawdowns from NHS frameworks and the current supplier/service performance at provider level? What interpreter coordination, if any, do nonprofit legal-aid providers themselves purchase?
11. For the US prospects, which entity is the purchaser, what is the language-services budget, and what is their current procurement/contract cycle? NYC Health + Hospitals’ RFP cannot answer those questions for NYU or legal aid.
12. Does any buyer want a coordination layer separate from an interpreting vendor’s portal or government service, and what would it replace rather than duplicate?

### Risks to avoid in subsequent discovery

- Treating provider availability or a public service commitment as proof of unmet demand.
- Treating interpreter shortage claims as measured prevalence without denominators and current local evidence.
- Treating annual spend, a contract ceiling, framework value or program funding as software budget or willingness to pay.
- Combining court interpretation, patient encounters, legal-aid casework and community navigation into one generic workflow.
- Assuming community clinics can buy centrally, or that every organization is eligible for government-funded TIS.
- Collecting identifiable patient/client case data during discovery or recording sensitive stories without explicit authorization and appropriate safeguards.
- Presenting this report as legal/privacy advice or assuming a single product design will satisfy four jurisdictions.

### Recommended next action

Conduct **separate** discovery tracks for healthcare/community and legal assistance. Recruit a balanced set of operational users, coordinators, managers, finance/procurement and privacy/IT stakeholders across at least two countries per track before making a segment or market decision. Start with anonymized walkthroughs of a recent completed request and a difficult/failed request; ask to see blank forms, process maps or redacted status reports rather than client records. Record what is directly observed separately from participant explanations and assumptions. Obtain permission, do not contact anyone without explicit authorization, and do not represent these ten prospects as willing participants.

A useful first comparison after interviews would be: (a) time/steps per request and exception; (b) frequency and consequence of failure; (c) extent to which existing systems already solve the coordination task; (d) named budget owner and procurement path; and (e) governance/integration burden. If interviews cannot identify a measurable, repeated coordination problem and an accountable buyer beyond existing interpreter-service procurement, pause the software opportunity rather than choosing an MVP prematurely.
