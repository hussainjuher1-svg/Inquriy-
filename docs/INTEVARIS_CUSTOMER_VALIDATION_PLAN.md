# INTEVARIS — customer problem validation plan

**Status: research plan; no MVP selected.** This document does not authorize backend work, a product launch, an integration or a sales commitment. The three directions are competing hypotheses, not established customer needs:

- **A:** Interpretation request and session management.
- **B:** Interpreter directory, qualifications and availability management.
- **C:** Interpretation governance, consent, audit and quality management.

The Stage 5 scope document suggested investigating A because it appeared bounded. **That is not market evidence and gives A no advantage in this research.** The same evidence standards apply to A, B and C. No interviews have been conducted for this plan; there are no INTEVARIS customer findings or fabricated scores below.

## 1. Research objective and evidence rules

Determine whether any one segment has a problem that is important enough, frequent enough **or sufficiently consequential when rare**, and poorly served enough by its current alternatives to justify a realistic first product. Identify an accessible buyer, credible resource or purchasing behavior, and security/operational constraints before choosing a direction.

**Labels used throughout**

- **DOCUMENTED:** a specific published source says this; it is not proof that all organizations behave this way. Vendor claims are self-reported, not independently verified.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** a possible pattern or problem that requires research. Segment labels, buyers and constraints below are hypotheses unless explicitly sourced.
- **UNKNOWN:** no verified INTEVARIS customer evidence is available.
- **PROPOSED:** a research method or decision rule to agree before collecting data.

**Evidence hierarchy, strongest first:** permissioned, redacted first-hand records or direct observation; a specific recent incident independently corroborated by another participant or artifact; detailed first-hand recollection with dates and quantities; generalized statements; and hypothetical enthusiasm. Record contradictions and missing artifacts. A vendor's feature list or listed price shows an offered alternative, **not** customer use, outcomes, demand for INTEVARIS, or willingness to buy it.

### Purchasing and willingness-to-pay signals

**PROPOSED:** Record these categories separately; none has been observed for INTEVARIS in this plan. They are not interchangeable or a guaranteed linear progression:

| Signal | What it establishes, and what it does not |
|---|---|
| **Verbal interest** | A participant says the idea is interesting; compliments, survey responses and hypothetical price answers do **not** establish intent or willingness to pay. |
| **Reported existing spending** | A participant describes past spending or staff effort for a related problem. Record amount, period and source as self-reported; it is not independently verified or proof they would buy INTEVARIS. |
| **Verified existing spending** | A permissioned, redacted invoice, contract or budget-to-actual record corroborates prior spending. It demonstrates payment for a past alternative, not future spend on INTEVARIS. Paid staff time demonstrates resources, not an external purchase. |
| **Approved budget** | A budget owner confirms an actual allocated amount and applicable purpose, period and approval conditions; corroborate with permissioned redacted documentation if possible. A general budget is not a commitment to INTEVARIS. |
| **Actual procurement activity** | An authorized buyer has initiated a real purchase process—such as a documented evaluation, procurement request or security review. Record scope, owner, stage and blockers; activity can stop without purchase. |
| **Paid pilot or purchase commitment** | An authorized organization signs a specific paid-pilot agreement or purchase commitment, or pays an invoice; distinguish a binding commitment from payment actually received and record scope, price, conditions and evidence. This is the strongest specific signal for that offer, not proof of broad demand or product success. |

**PROPOSED:** Establish who has authority, which organization the signal concerns, whether the offer is INTEVARIS or an existing alternative, evidence provenance, currency, date and conditions. Do not imply that an interview participant is a buyer or solicit commitments before an offer and the appropriate approvals exist. Failure to obtain a commitment is not proof of no demand when no offer has been made.

The Stage 4 discovery found no verified INTEVARIS backend in the accessible sources, not proof that none exists elsewhere. Current public and workspace routes, their disconnected states and the unvalidated local scorer are described in `docs/INTEVARIS_MVP_SCOPE_OPTIONS.md`. Interface labels do not validate a workflow.

## 5. Interview and recruitment protocol

**PROPOSED sample, not research already performed:** recruit approximately 18–24 interviews across the six potential segments, including buyers, coordinators and independent interpreters. Seek varied organization size, geography, language modalities and scheduled versus on-demand work; include people satisfied with current solutions and those who recently declined or switched tools. The target is a planning heuristic, not a representative statistical sample. Some participants may cover more than one role. A decision for one segment requires enough **independent organizations in that same segment**; do not treat interviews within one organization as independent buyers.

**PROPOSED method:** 30–45 minutes; obtain informed consent, explain the research is not a sales pitch, allow skipping and withdrawal, and offer appropriate accessibility accommodations and fair compensation if available. Recruit through opt-in professional associations, publicly available organization contacts and referrals; diversify sources to reduce referral bias. Do not contact anyone automatically or use private records. Record interview date, role, setting, modality and recruitment source, then ask about the most recent actual case and a recent exception. Ask for a second example only after the first. Ask for permitted, redacted evidence rather than names, patient/client details, protected case details, credentials, contact information or recordings of interpretation.

**PROPOSED privacy and consent:** before taking notes, explain their purpose, what will be retained, who can access them, the planned retention/deletion process and the participant's ability to skip or withdraw; obtain and document informed consent for note-taking. Without consent, do not take or retain notes. No recording or screenshots by default; obtain separate explicit consent before any recording and separately before viewing or retaining an artifact. Participants must have authorization and redact private or privileged content themselves; do not collect patient details, legal case details, credentials or interpretation content. They may describe the process instead. Stop and do not copy material if unexpected confidential information appears. Store permitted notes in an access-controlled location selected by the research owner; agree retention, deletion and access before collection, including what withdrawal can practically remove. Do not place raw interviews, confidential material or contact lists in the repository. Do not ask participants to violate employer, professional or client confidentiality obligations.

**PROPOSED role verification:** an interviewee is a research participant, not automatically a prospective customer or buyer. Record whether they are a daily user, recommender, budget holder, purchaser or approver and how their authority was established. Distinguish observed purchasing action from an account of someone else's authority. Make no legal or compliance claims based on the interview.

### Shared opening and neutral probes

- “What is your role in language-access work, and what is outside your responsibility?”
- “Tell me about the most recent time this work came up. What triggered it, and what happened next?”
- “Which people and tools were involved? Where did information move or get entered again?”
- “What happened after that? How do you know the outcome?”
- “Was this typical? What was different in the most recent case that did not go as planned?”
- “How often did something like this happen during a period you can estimate? What evidence supports that estimate?”
- “What did it cost in time, money or service impact, if known? What would have happened otherwise?”
- “What works well about your current process? When is this not a problem?”

### Buyer guide

1. “Walk me through the last decision to buy, renew, change or reject a tool or service related to language access. What triggered it?”
2. “Which alternatives were evaluated, what was selected, and why? What did the existing alternative already do well?”
3. “Who requested the purchase, controlled the budget, approved it, and could block it? What evidence did each person require?”
4. “What was actually spent or budgeted, including staff time, service contracts, software, setup and switching? Share a range or say you cannot disclose it.”
5. “When was the last operational incident important enough to receive management attention? What was its consequence and how was it measured?”
6. “What security, confidentiality, accessibility, contractual or procurement review did the last purchase require? How long did that process actually take?”
7. “What problem has not been worth buying for, and why? What would make a new purchase unnecessary?”

### Coordinator / operational user guide

1. “Describe the most recent request from first notice to outcome. Who did each step and where was it recorded?”
2. “What information was needed at each handoff? Which parts were missing, copied or corrected in that case?”
3. “Tell me about the last time an interpreter or service was unavailable, late, cancelled or changed. What did you do and who resolved it?”
4. “How did you determine whether someone was qualified and available? What source did you trust, and when was it last checked?”
5. “Tell me about the latest review, complaint, consent record or audit request you personally handled, if any. What evidence was needed and where was it?”
6. “How much active work and waiting time did the last case involve? Is there a permitted aggregate or redacted record we can compare with your estimate?”
7. “Which existing tool or step would you keep unchanged? When is the current process sufficient?”

### Interpreter guide

1. “Tell me about your most recent assignment from offer to completion. How did the opportunity reach you and how did you respond?”
2. “What information did you receive, where did you keep it, and what did you have to ask for or enter again?”
3. “Describe the last scheduling change or conflict. Who contacted whom, and how was it resolved?”
4. “How did you last provide or update language, availability or qualification information? Who checked it and how long did it take?”
5. “What happened during the last issue, feedback request or dispute you can discuss without confidential details? Who had authority to resolve it?”
6. “What tools or services do you personally pay for or invest time in? What did you last stop using, and why?”
7. “What assignment information should never be shared with an additional platform? What consent or organizational permissions would you need before showing a redacted example?”

**PROPOSED moderator discipline:** do not ask “Would you use our app?” as primary evidence; avoid naming the proposed solution until after reconstructing actual behavior. Follow up on costs and alternatives without implying that a particular problem occurred. Do not ask for protected encounter content or infer a legal obligation from an interview.

## 8. Decision framework and go/no-go gates

**PROPOSED provisional decision guidelines**, subject to product-owner agreement before interviews: the five-organization, three-episode and two-authority thresholds below are practical minimums for a qualitative review, **not statistically representative samples, proof of demand, validated effect sizes or automatic pass/fail rules**. Record the agreed rationale and evidence quality. Even if every count is met, seek additional research if the sample is concentrated in referrals, regions or organization types; authority or evidence cannot be corroborated; important counterexamples or high-risk/rare events remain unexplained; procurement or security conditions are unresolved; or sources conflict. Fewer observations can inform a hypothesis but do not by themselves establish a decision-ready case.

1. **Decision-ready sample:** for the leading direction in a specifically defined segment, gather evidence from at least **five independent organizations or independent solo practices**, including at least **two people with actual purchasing or budget authority**. Capture non-buyer users separately. Recruit beyond referrals where possible. If this is not reached, record **insufficient evidence**, not failure or success.
2. **Problem and consequence:** at least **three independent entities** describe a specific relevant past episode with a corroborating permitted artifact, direct observation or another independent first-hand account where feasible. Record the observation period and whether the issue is recurring. For a rare but severe problem, require a credible documented consequence and explicit owner rationale instead of pretending high frequency. Capture cases that contradict the pattern.
3. **Existing alternatives and resources:** provisionally seek verifiable evidence from at least **two independent entities** of actual prior resource allocation or spending related to the problem, and explanations from at least two relevant decision-makers of their real purchasing process and constraints. Separate reported from corroborated spending and use the signal categories in section 1. Paid staff time and spending on an existing alternative show resources, **not** willingness to buy INTEVARIS. Vendor prices, compliments, survey answers or hypothetical price interest do not meet this gate. Approved budget, real procurement activity and any paid pilot or purchase commitment should be recorded separately, with offer, authority and conditions; none is assumed. If there is no specific approved offer, lack of a paid commitment is not negative evidence.
4. **Differentiation and feasibility:** compare the proposed narrow job with the actual alternatives for those entities, including doing nothing. Document why a new solution might be worth switching to, plus the smallest feasible scope and unresolved security, confidentiality and operating dependencies. Do not infer an unmet need merely because an incumbent has many features.
5. **Approval gate:** before architecture or implementation, the product owner reviews all evidence and counterexamples, explicitly selects or rejects a direction and segment, and obtains appropriate security and domain/legal input. A positive research gate is permission to make a decision, not permission to build or deploy.

**Support A** if repeated, independently corroborated request-to-outcome coordination failures and measurable consequences are observed, responsible buyers allocate resources to that problem, an external fulfillment process exists, and a bounded coordination tool improves on the actual alternatives. Test the null: the provider's own portal or on-demand service is enough, or manual records are already adequate.

**Support B** if independent entities repeatedly incur meaningful costs or operational failures from maintaining language, qualification or availability information, have a credible verification owner and source of truth, and actual procurement evidence supports a narrowly scoped solution. Test the null: agency/provider rosters or existing software already keep information current enough.

**Support C** if independent entities demonstrate consequential evidence, review or governance work that is poorly served today, identify authorized reviewers and purchasers, and can define safe, legitimate data and evidence handling. Test the null: existing systems or organizational procedures satisfy the need, or the problem cannot be addressed responsibly without unavailable evidence or legal clarity.

**Combine or narrow** only when the same underlying episode and buyer consistently span two directions and participants demonstrate the handoff cannot reasonably be solved separately; define a single bounded outcome rather than bundling modules. If different buyers, data owners or purchasing processes drive the problems, keep them separate. **PROPOSED examples to test:** request tracking plus a minimal externally confirmed interpreter reference, or credential-expiry review for a specific workforce. Neither is an approved feature.

**No-go / abandon all three:** stop pursuing these directions for the researched segment if evidence shows no meaningful unsolved problem, an inaccessible or nonexistent buyer, adequate low-cost alternatives with no reason to switch, inability to handle data safely, or dependencies that cannot realistically be met. A stop applies to the defined segment and evidence window; it is not proof about every possible market. Record the reason and consider fresh problem discovery without forcing a different option.

**Continue research, not go/no-go:** use when the sample, buyer access, evidence quality, security analysis or counterexamples are insufficient or conflicting. Do not convert missing data into a zero score, a pass or a claim of demand.
